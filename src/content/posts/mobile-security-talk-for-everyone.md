Your phone is your wallet, your ID and your photo album. I recently gave a 45-minute talk (plus 15 minutes of Q&A) on mobile security to a non-technical audience of adults who rarely think about it. The talk focuses on **Android**, with the **iPhone** equivalent next to every setting.

The core message is that most attacks are not sophisticated: they exploit urgency, trust and settings nobody reviewed. A few habits prevent the vast majority of them.

## Table of contents

## Three habits that change everything

1. **Update** the system and apps. Updates close flaws that criminals already know.
2. **Distrust urgency.** If something rushes you or asks for a code, hang up and call the official number yourself.
3. **Turn on two-step verification.** Even if your password is stolen, they can't get in without the second step.

## How people get tricked

- Links in SMS, WhatsApp or email asking for data.
- Fake calls from "your bank" asking for codes.
- The "child in trouble": a new number asking for an urgent transfer.
- Malicious apps installed from outside the official store.
- Stolen phone numbers (codes you never requested).
- A lost or stolen phone protected by an easy PIN.

Other frequent scams: fake payment receipts (a screenshot is not a payment: check your own banking app), miracle investments, fake online shops, spyware installed by someone with access to your phone, cloned profiles, and fake prizes or jobs that ask for advances or ID photos.

**The golden rule: no code is ever shared. Ever.** Not with the bank, not with the messaging app, not with a relative by message. No legitimate company asks for it.

## A family code word

Agree on a word **in person**, never in writing. If a relative asks for money urgently by message or audio, ask for the word. It stops the "child in trouble" scam even when the voice has been cloned, because the scammer doesn't know it. It costs nothing and it works.

## Passwords, two-step verification and recovery

- **One password per site.** If one small site leaks, attackers try that password on your email, bank and social networks.
- **Use a password manager.** It stores everything encrypted, generates long unique passwords and won't autofill on fake sites (a useful alarm). Options: the manager built into your phone, Bitwarden, 1Password or Proton Pass.
- **One memorized passphrase** for the manager: four or five unrelated words. Where available, prefer **passkeys**, which replace the password with your phone unlock.
- **Not all 2FA is equal:** SMS is better than nothing but vulnerable to number theft; app-based codes are better; a physical key or passkey resists phishing.
- **Enable it first** where it matters most: email/Google account, then your messaging app, online banking, digital wallets and social networks. If your email falls, everything else falls.
- **Print your recovery codes** and keep them on paper or inside the password manager. Never as a screenshot in your gallery.

## A 10-minute Android checklist

1. PIN of 6+ digits
2. Auto-lock after 30 seconds
3. Automatic updates
4. Play Protect enabled
5. Find My Device enabled
6. Theft protection enabled
7. "Install unknown apps" disabled
8. Privacy dashboard reviewed
9. Sensitive notifications hidden
10. Cloud backup enabled

Three things never to enable: installing unknown apps, accessibility permissions for dubious apps (banking trojans use them to read your screen and operate on their own), and sharing codes.

## Separating what is sensitive

- **Secure Folder / Private Space:** "a locked room inside your phone" for photos, IDs and medical documents. It has its own PIN, and if you forget it, it can't be recovered.
- **Work profile:** two phones in one. Work apps live apart from personal data, and the company only sees the work side. Free apps such as Shelter create a profile of your own, handy to test a dubious app in isolation.
- **On iPhone** there is no single equivalent, but there are locked albums, per-app Face ID locks (iOS 18+), locked notes and Safari profiles.

## If something goes wrong: cut, change, notify

- **Cut:** airplane mode, uninstall the suspicious app, block the line and the device.
- **Change:** passwords from another device, starting with your Google account and email, and close open sessions.
- **Notify:** bank and wallets immediately, your contacts by another channel, and file a police report.

If you gave a code by mistake, there is no shame: it happens to everyone. Call the bank right away, because the first minutes matter most. Write down your bank's and carrier's official numbers today, on paper too.

## Your plan for this week

1. Update the phone and apps.
2. Never share a code.
3. Turn on 2FA for Google and your messaging app.
4. Keep recovery codes on paper.
5. Write down the emergency numbers.

Then, little by little, add a password manager and a secure folder. And when in doubt: ask before you tap.
